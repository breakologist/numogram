# Brébeuf, Jogues and the martyrs: living customs and contested memory

This is a companion page to [[brebeuf-jogues]], by the same author. It asks whether it is too soon for folk practice to have grown up around these saints. The short answer is no, but most of what exists is shrine-led devotion. The most clearly *folk* tradition is the Wendat community's own singing of the carol, and it is ambivalent. Quotations are copied from the fetched pages.

## (a) Verified living customs

- **The carol at Wendake.** At l'Église Notre-Dame-de-Lorette, "Today the song is still sung in Wendat at the church, where it is afforded a special place in Christmas masses, usually as the communion or the opening hymn." The organist says: "I hear the lyrics, but I don't understand them" (Broadview, 2018). The first known reference is Le Mercier's account of a dying girl, Therese, on Christmas Eve 1668: "an air sung by the Hurons on Christmas Festivals." A local Catholic, Claude Vincent, says: "All my aunts sang that song when I was young". The carol was "passed down orally" in the community, and "A Jesuit priest wrote down the Wendat words in the late 18th century".
- **Re-indigenised versions.** The Wendat poet Andrée Levesque Sioui wrote new words for *Iesous ahatonnia'*, presented in Wendake in October 2024. She calls it "this decolonized version" (CBC, 24 December 2024). Bishop Mark MacDonald describes children acting out the carol in Indigenous churches, with the kings "all dressed in traditional Navajo attire" (Broadview).
- **Summer pilgrimage to Auriesville.** The first Mass was on 15 August 1885 with "Four thousand pilgrims". "Local parishes adopted the practice of visiting Auriesville during the summer, especially on the significant anniversary of the Feast of the Assumption" (shrine history page).
- **Walking pilgrimages and ethnic pilgrimages at Midland.** The shrine says: "More than 35 ethnic and cultural pilgrimage events take place each summer. Week-long walking pilgrimages are organized from Toronto and other locations." (martyrs-shrine.com)

## (b) Devotional and official practice

- **The skull: verified, and divided.** The Augustines' archive describes a silver bust-reliquary by Charles de Poilly, "Vers 1664-1665", commissioned by the Brébeuf family. In 1925 the skull was split between the Jesuits of Quebec and the Augustines of the Hôtel-Dieu, and the operation took place "au bloc opératoire de l'Hôtel-Dieu de Québec". Wax facsimiles were made so that each half would look like a whole skull. The bust holds "la moitié droite du crâne". The Augustines' annals for 1665 record that Marie-Catherine de Saint-Augustin "utilise les reliques de Jean de Brébeuf pour convertir un patient".
- **The relic tour, 2024–26.** The Midland relics are Brébeuf's skull, bones of Lalemant and Garnier, and a relic of Kateri Tekakwitha. They went on tour so that people could "receive the graces of healing and reconciliation". Visitors "press their holy cards to the reliquaries and bring the home with them as spiritual keepsakes (and third-class relics)" (martyrs-shrine.com). The shrine also says: "Many healings have been reported and documented." *Caveat:* this is the shrine's own claim. I found no independent documentation.
- **Free entry for Indigenous visitors.** Since 2021–22, "all First Nations, Metis and Inuit people have free access to the shrine in perpetuity" (shrine director Fr Michael Knox, *Catholic Register*, 24 September 2022).
- **Auriesville changed hands; it was not sold.** "on March 22, 2017, the Society of Jesus generously transferred the deed for the main campus of the holy ground to 'the Friends.'" (shrine history). What was *sold* was the former retreat house, bought for $600,000 by the "American Sports Committee Inc." (*The Evangelist*, URL dated 2018).
- **Kateri Tekakwitha.** Her canonisation in 2012 rested on the Finkbonner healing of 2006 (Wikipedia). Long before that there was clear folk devotion: some people "collected earth from her gravesite and wore it in bags as a relic" (Wikipedia, citing Greer, *Mohawk Saint*). The Auriesville shrine claims her birthplace, and Midland says "The home of her relic is at the Martyrs' Shrine."

## (c) Folk-looking claims that are unsourced or false

- **Brébeuf named lacrosse after a bishop's crosier.** Not supported. *The Canadian Encyclopedia*: "Although some believe the game was so named because the stick resembled a bishop's 'crosier,' other scholars point out that by this time the French used the expression jouer à la crosse to refer to any game played with a curved stick and ball." The first printed use of the name is in the 1630s Huron reports.
- **Healing at Midland.** The only source is the shrine's own statement. I found no case files.
- **Jesuit school customs and place names** (Brébeuf College School, Jogues parishes, Lake George and so on). I did not research these, so no claims are made.
- **Where Ossernenon actually was.** The shrine is defending its location against Richard Upsher Smith Jr.'s 2025 book, which puts the martyrdoms "about seven miles away … at what archeologists call the Bauder Site" (shrine rebuttal page). Wikipedia cites Dean Snow for the Bauder site. The pilgrimage may therefore be fixed on a place chosen by 19th-century tradition, not by archaeology.

## (d) Indigenous perspectives

- **Wendat (Wendake).** Michel Savard, a Huron-Wendat museum curator, reads the carol's line about the spirit "who held us prisoner" as a condemnation of Wendat spirituality: "Christianity destroyed a part of us." He adds: "I have a feeling it has been more important outside of our nation." The linguist Arakwa Sioui says "Our priority right now is not [Jesous Ahatonnia]" and that it "is not a part of our real culture". Yet she expects the community to keep singing it "to make our ancestors live on" (Broadview, 2018).
- **Mohawk (Kanien'kehá:ka), on Kateri.** As quoted on Wikipedia from the Associated Press, 2012: Orenda Boucher speaks of "mixed feelings". Doug George-Kanentiio says her sainthood "should never obscure the best elements of our aboriginal spirituality". Chaz Kader objects to "the contrast of good Mohawks and bad Mohawks". *Caveat:* these are secondary quotations. I did not fetch the AP original.
- **Shrine framing.** Midland frames the martyrs as "fitting saints to pray to" for reconciliation. It says there is "no formal connection" between the tour and the Truth and Reconciliation process.
- **Not found:** a formal statement by the Huron-Wendat Nation council, or by Mohawk councils (Kahnawà:ke, Akwesasne, Kanatsiohareke), about the martyrs or the shrines.
