# St Luke the Evangelist in the *Golden Legend* (feast: 18 October)

*Research note for the daily reading of [[reading-2026-10-18]]. by Em (in collaboration with the user), 2026-10-18.*

**Caveats up front.** (1) This chapter contains **no painter story, no death and no relics**. (2) The Welsh links below are loose; no Welsh St Luke's Day custom was found. ## Source and edition

- **Work:** Jacobus de Voragine, *Legenda Aurea* (compiled c. 1260s); Latin critical edition by Th. Graesse (Leipzig, 1850), where the chapter is *De sancto Luca evangelista*.
- **Text used here:** William Caxton's English version (1483), modernised by F. S. Ellis, *The Golden Legend or Lives of the Saints*, Temple Classics, 1900 (vol. 6, "The Life of S. Luke", p. 23). Etext scanned by Robert Blackmon for Paul Halsall's Internet Medieval Sourcebook.

  (index: https://sourcebooks.fordham.edu/basis/goldenlegend/)

## Structure of the chapter

The chapter is almost all exegesis and allegory. There is little biography in it, and there is **no account of Luke's death, burial or relics, and no painter tradition**. Jacobus's portrait of Luke is organised around a four-fold scheme, with three-fold schemes nested inside it.

### 1. The name (etymology)

> "Luke is as much to say as arising or enhancing himself. Or Luke is said of light"

He rose "from the love of the world" into "the love of God". He was also "light of the world", which leads into a run of solar comparisons from Scripture: the sun has height, sweetness ("The light of the sun is sweet") and swiftness. The section then turns these into four qualities of Luke:

> "Luke had highness by the love of things celestial, delectable by sweet conversation, swiftness by fervent predication and utility, and profit by conscription and writing of his doctrine."

### 2. Life (brief)

> "Luke was of the nation of Syria, and Antiochian by art of medicine"

Jacobus sets out the debate over whether Luke was one of the seventy-two disciples. He reports Jerome's view that Luke was a "disciple of the apostles and not of our Lord" and concludes "it is more to be holden that he was none of the seventy-two". Luke is called a virgin who "had never wife ne children" (citing Jerome). He is Paul's faithful companion: "Luke is only with me" (2 Tim. 4). Some identified him with Cleophas's unnamed companion on the road to Emmaus, and he "named not his name for meekness".

### 3. The master scheme: four "ordinances" and four faces (Ezekiel 1)

Luke was "well ordained as toward God, and as touching his neighbour, as touching himself, and as touching his office". As a sign of this, "he was described to have four faces, that is to wit, the face of a man, the face of a lion, the face of an ox and the face of an eagle". Jacobus asks us to imagine a beast with a four-square head, a face on each side, and "every corner was a pen" (a feather or wing).

- The four evangelists' symbols, following Jerome: Matthew is the man, Mark the lion, John the eagle, and Luke the ox. Christ himself is all four: "a man born of the virgin, he was an ox in his passion, a lion in his resurrection, and an eagle in his ascension."
- How the faces map onto Luke's ordinances:
  - **Man**: towards his neighbour, because man is "a beast reasonable, debonair, and liberal".
  - **Eagle**: towards God. This section includes the bestiary story of the eagle that, scorched by the sun, plunges into a fountain and "taketh away the darkness of his eyes".
  - **Lion**: towards himself (noblesse, subtlety, sufferance). The lion "defaceth his traces and steps with his tail".
  - **Ox**: towards his office of writing the gospel.

### 4. The ox symbolism

> "Luke was figured in the ox, for he devised about the priesthood of .Jesu Christ."

> "The ox is a moral beast and hath his foot cloven, by which is discretion understood, and it is a beast sacrificeable."

The ox stands for how Luke wrote. He "proceeded morally", starting from the nativity "and so proceeded little and little". He wrote "discreetly", after the other two evangelists, adding what they had left out. And he was learned in "the sacrifices and works of the temple". The cloven foot comes back later: "he was figured in the form of an ox, which hath the foot cloven, by which the virtue of discretion is expressed".

### 5. Nested threefold and fourfold schemes

- **Towards God** (after Bernard): his affection was holy, his thought clean and his intention rightful.
- **Towards his neighbour** (after Richard of St Victor, with a fourth item added): his power, knowledge, will and deeds. His words "be medicine unto a sick soul" (Jerome).
- **Towards himself** (after Bernard): sober living, rightful work and a debonair wit, each of them divided into three.
- **His office (the Gospel)**: "ennoblished with much truth, it is full of much profit, it is embellished with much honesty and authorised by great authority".
  - **Truth**: three truths, "of life, of righteousness, and of doctrine".
  - **Profit**: a "treble medicine, curing, preserving, and amending", made ready by "the leech celestial".
  - **Honesty of style**, after Augustine: "that it please, that it appear and move".
  - **Authority**: eight authorisations: by the Father, the Son, the Holy Ghost, the angels, the prophets (Ezekiel's ox), the Virgin, the apostles and Paul.

### 6. Notable passages

- The Virgin as his source: "the blessed Luke had recourse to her like as to the ark of the Testament, and was certified of her many things". This is the nearest the chapter comes to the later tradition of Luke and the Virgin, but nothing in it mentions painting.
- Ezekiel's book as Luke's Gospel: "written within for to hide the mystery of profoundness, and without for the showing of the history".
- The witnesses (Augustine): John and Matthew are witnesses "of things seen", while Mark and Luke are witnesses of "things heard".
- Darkness at the Passion (from the Ambrose passage): "the sun had withdrawn her light and rays".
- **Closing miracle (the "history of Antioch")**: the Christians are besieged by Turks and suffering hunger. Once they repent, "a man full of clearness in white vestment" appears in the church of Our Lady at Tripoli, "he said that he was Luke that came from Antioch", where the Lord had gathered "the chivalry of heaven". The Christians then "discomfited all the host of the Turks".

## Welsh connection to St Luke's Day

I found **no reliable source for a distinctively Welsh folk custom on St Luke's Day**. What I did find:

- The Church in Wales calendar lists Luc, Efengylwr (Luke, Evangelist) on 18 Hydref (18 October) (https://www.churchinwales.org.uk/cy/cyhoeddiadau/gair-yr-arglwydd/dyddiau-sanctaidd/).
- Welsh Wikipedia (*Luc*) mentions the Luke portrait in the *Book of St Chad / Llyfr Teilo*, which it describes as made in south Wales in the second half of the 8th century. Scholars dispute where the book was made, and this is a connection to St Luke rather than to his day.
- A Clayton & Bell window (c. 1901) at Llangasty Tal-y-llyn, Powys, shows "St Luke the beloved physician". It commemorates Robert Raikes, who was born on St Luke's Day 1818 (Imaging the Bible in Wales database, https://imagingthebible.llgc.org.uk/object/4624).

The well-known St Luke's Day customs I found are English, for example Charlton Horn Fair in Kent and Whip-dog Day in York.

## Threads for the numogram reading (SPECULATIVE)

See [[reading-2026-10-18]] for the full reading and the gate arithmetic.

*These are interpretive associations, not claims about the text or about Jacobus's intentions. The zone numbers are as supplied in the brief.*

- **Light and its eclipse ([[tai-hsuan-ching|Tai Xuan]] 67 Darkening; [[hexagram-zone-mapping|I Ching]] 36 Ming Yi, "Darkening of the Light").** The chapter opens by deriving *Luke* from *lux*: "light of the world", the sun with "height", "sweet" and "swift". That makes Luke's day a natural counterweight to a reading about light obscured. Three passages fit Ming Yi's theme of light hidden under adversity closely:
  - the Passion, when "the sun had withdrawn her light and rays";
  - the Gospel as a book "written within for to hide the mystery of profoundness";
  - the eagle that removes "the darkness of his eyes" by plunging into water.
  The Antioch siege story, with light ("full of clearness in white vestment") appearing to people besieged and starving, reads almost as an allegory of Ming Yi's counsel to persevere in darkness.
- **Three and four ([[zone-3]]/[[zone-4]]; [[zone-5]]/[[zone-9]]).** The chapter is literally built of nested threes inside an overarching four: four faces, four ordinances, four "pens" per beast, set against treble verity, treble medicine and Bernard's triads. That echoes the tetragram's split readings (zone 3 by line value, zone 4 by number). The four-faced, four-square beast may also suggest the *tetra-*gram itself.
- **Libra III and the 4 of Swords.** In the Golden Dawn system, Libra III / 4 of Swords is traditionally titled Lord of Rest from Strife (not checked against a source in this research): truce and convalescence, and sits under the scales of Libra. The ox's cloven foot signifying "discretion" (a dividing, weighing virtue) suits Libra. Luke the "leech", whose words "be medicine unto a sick soul", and his "treble medicine, curing, preserving, and amending", suit the convalescent stillness of the 4 of Swords. The Antioch episode, a siege lifted after repentance, also maps onto strife followed by respite.
- **St Luke's Little Summer.** In weather lore this is a short warm or fine spell from about 18 October. The *Guardian*'s Weatherwatch (13 Oct 2016) says it was reputed to last from 18 to 28 October, and The Times cites a study of 1889–1940 records giving a 67% chance of good weather. A brief return of sunlight as the year darkens fits Jacobus's solar etymology ("The light of the sun is sweet") and makes a gentle counter-image to 36/67: light that is withdrawn but not extinguished.
