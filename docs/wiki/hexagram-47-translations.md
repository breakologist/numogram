# Hexagram 47 困 Kun: translations side by side

**Wen's framing notes.** The trigrams are Lake and Water, and the elements Metal 金 and Water 水. The character 困 is Wood 木 "boxed in and surrounded 口". When 47 comes up, "increase Wood *qi* in your surroundings". The Thunder trigram is used against the blockade: changing the fifth line from yang to yin turns the upper Lake into Thunder and gives hexagram 40 解 Jie, release, untying the knots. She also says 47 "was traditionally used in baneful magic to blockade someone" and keep them from prospering.

### Wen's glosses (summarised)

- **幽 yōu**: concealed, imprisoned, deep and remote, or the netherworld. **谷 gu**: a valley or gorge between two mountains. Together they are a figure for being trapped in a difficult predicament.
- **歲 Sui**: Jupiter and the god **Tai Sui**, and so years. Each 歲 is also one harvest, so 三歲 can be read as "three harvests". Tai Sui/Jupiter is linked with Wood, the 木 that is blockaded by 口 in 困.
- **Line 2**: the sash is where officials hung their seals. It can stand for government, law and institutions, and it may foreshadow punishment. The offering can mean reflection, or asking the ancestors for counsel.
- **Line 3**: she gives two readings of the missing wife. Either there is no one to depend on, or the wife has been kidnapped, so something valuable has to be recovered. Wen's psychological gloss: man = logic, wife = intuition. Extenuating, possibly "maliciously cast" forces have to be counteracted.
- **Line 4**: a question of identity ("Who am I?"). In a lighter mundane reading, the golden carriage stands for travel delays.
- **Line 5**: she lists the Five Punishments 五刑: 墨 tattooing the face, 劓 cutting off the nose, 刖 cutting off the feet, 宮 castration, 大辟 execution. The nose stands for one's luck, and the feet were cut for theft, trespass, treason or sedition. In her narrative, the punishment that line 2 foreshadowed is carried out here. **祭 jì** is going before the altar with an offering; the glyph is an altar with a hand holding meat. **祀 sì** means both to sacrifice and to venerate.
- **Line 6**: the "expedition" (征) suggests a strike with the aim of conquest, an ambitious undertaking, and it is auspicious.

## D'Aoust, *The Occult I Ching* (pp. 303–307)

D'Aoust does not translate the line texts. She gives her own reading of the hexagram and gives the six lines her own names (Self, Other, Union, Earth, Conflict, Heaven), so her section has no place in the per-line Chinese table above. It is summarised here, with short quotes.

- **Header.** "Hexagram 47 / The Secret Garden / Kun". There is an oracle-script glyph labelled **"Magic Circle"**: a branching, tree-like figure inside a rounded rectangle, the 木 in 口 again. Keywords: **"Circumscribed"**, **Happiness in Slavery**, **Oppression**. **Tarot Card: Four of Cups.**
- **Her thesis.** Most people read 47 as punishment or prison. After years with it, she thinks it is "making fun of" that view, "poking fun at the pity party". The attitude "is creating the prison". She compares it to Christ on the cross and to the South Netherlandish tapestry ***The Unicorn in Captivity***, "a unicorn tied to a tree and circled by a fence". In both cases the suffering lies in adopting "a woe-is-me attitude". She also cites Siddhartha under the Bodhi tree, who faced the world's evils without breaking. Her advice: seek justice, but don't fall into "a blame game", and rest in your own innate value.
- **Illustration (p. 304, full page).** A white line drawing on a dark ground: one standing figure, with a second head or face set above and to one side of its own, inside two vertically overlapping circles (a vesica-like shape). The epigraph is from Odell Shepard, *The Lore of the Unicorn*: "The unconquerable nature of God is likened to that of a unicorn." The p. 303 sentence "…can endure almost" continues "anything." on p. 305.
- **Poem.** Lord Byron, "When Coldness Wraps This Suffering Clay", reprinted in full (public domain). It opens "When coldness wraps this suffering clay, / Ah! whither strays the immortal mind?"
- **Guru.** The lesson is to be content in oneself whatever the circumstances. The adept makes "a secret safe place of peace inside the heart, her own Garden of Eden", a state of *samadhi* that no one can take away; "you can be defeated but you can never be conquered."
- **Personal example.** A client asked only, "How about the business I just started?" She drew 47 and saw "a plant growing within an enclosure", an indoor or secret garden. She asked whether he was growing marijuana; he was, and "We both started to laugh."
- **Changing lines.** Each ends "Transforms into Shadow":
  1. *The Self.* Confinement shared with others, "Many hands make the burden lighter". → Shadow **58.1**
  2. *The Other.* Seclusion imposed by an authority figure. Use the time constructively, and if the authority is corrupt, stop following that person. → **45.2**
  3. *The Union.* Not seeing how valuable a supporting partner or friend is; don't "bite the hand that feeds you". → **28.3**
  4. *The Earth.* Freed from oppression, let it go and "Lay your burden down by the riverside", like spring-cleaning. → **29.4**
  5. *The Conflict.* Oppression's lessons; take pride in surviving hard times. → **40.5**
  6. *Heaven.* The trap is self-imposed, "a mind box"; look inside it and "the walls simply disintegrate." → **6.6**
  - *All lines changing → hexagram 22*: mercy. In the deepest despair, "the Mother of All sends the sweet grace of mercy"; to give mercy is to lift the lost "out of purgatory".
- **The Shadow hexagrams are the single-line changes (checked).** Take 47 from bottom to top as ⚋⚊⚋⚊⚊⚋ (Kan below, Dui above). Flipping one line gives: line 1 → Dui/Dui = **58**; line 2 → Kun/Dui = **45**; line 3 → Xun/Dui = **28**; line 4 → Kan/Kan = **29**; line 5 → Kan/Zhen = **40**; line 6 → Kan/Qian = **6**. Flipping all six gives Li below, Gen above = **22**. All seven match her numbers, so "Shadow N.k" means "the hexagram reached by changing line k". (Wen's 5th-line change to 40 Jie agrees.)

## Huang, *The Complete I Ching* (pp. 374–381)

His line texts are in the per-line table above, under "Others". Short quotes only; the rest is summarised.

- **Name and glyph.** Kun means "difficulty and hardship", and anciently being surrounded by enemies, poverty-stricken or exhausted. The ideograph is "a tree placed within a big mouth, kou. Here 'mouth' signifies an enclosure." *Before the Zhou dynasty*, he says, the glyph was "wood and the sign for stop", wood that stops growing. The preceding gua (46 Sheng) is wood growing upward; the sequence says that growing upward without stopping ends in exhaustion. Water below Lake is "a picture of an empty lake".
- **Translators compared.** Wilhelm "Oppression (Exhaustion)", Blofeld "Adversity, Weariness", Huang "Exhausting".
- **Decision.** "There are words / Not believed." In the commentary: "He who considers the words important / Falls into exhaustion."
- **朱紱 zhu fu, "scarlet kneecaps".** *Zhu* is bright red. *Fu* "has two meanings": "the silk ribbon for fastening an official seal", or an imperial robe worn at ceremonial offerings, "a robe for covering the knees when bending and kowtowing". Only high officials wore it.
- **King Wen.** The southern expedition met difficulties; the Duke of Zhou narrates the hardship; the people would not believe the truth, so King Wen "twice performed a sacrificial ceremony" (lines 2 and 5) and reached the end.
- **Line notes.**
  - **Line 1.** Normally the bottom line is the toes; here it is the buttocks, "on the bottom when one is sitting". The person is stuck like a stump, for "a long period of time (not necessarily three years)".
  - **Line 2.** Exhaustion "from excess"; feasting robes are fit for sacrifice but not daily life. Only in 47 and **9 Little Accumulation** do lines 2 and 5 not correspond "and yet there is still good fortune".
  - **Line 3.** He quotes Confucius's Great Treatise: "Exhausted in a situation where one should not be exhausted … How can he see his wife?"
  - **Line 4.** The prince rode a golden carriage to greet the bride. Kan below is wheels and a carriage, Lake above a youngest daughter, Water a middle son: "the most joyful time in one's life, yet he still feels exhausted."
  - **Line 5.** "There are two primary types of exhaustion—from insufficiency or from excess." Line 5 is neither: "his aspirations cannot be fulfilled". He is hemmed in by the yin lines 3 and 6, high officials whose knees look scarlet in imperial robes, and is restricted as if nose and feet were cut off.
  - **Line 6.** "the end of the road—the turning point is at hand."
- **Significance.** "A lake without water dries out. A man without money breaks down." He adds the proverb that a man without money "is like a fish with no water". The virtuous exhaust themselves "with the Tao", forgetting food and sleep.
- **Reference box (p. 381).** Lake above, Water below; Element **Metal**; three yang with three yin; Month "The ninth month of the lunar calendar, or October"; Host lines Second Nine and Fifth Nine; Opposite **22** Adorning; Inverse **48** Replenishing; Mutual **37** Household.

## Karcher, *Total I Ching* (pp. 340–345)

His line texts are in the per-line table above, after Huang's. Each line opens "Confined and Oppressed…". Short quotes only; the rest is summarised.

- **Glyph.** "KUN shows a tree MU (1) in an enclosure WEI (2) or old house": 木 inside 囗 (*wei*, "enclosure"), not 口 (*kou*, "mouth"). The gloss calls it "a big mouth or ramshackle house that threatens to engulf it". His long list of senses runs from enclosed and persecuted through penal codes and prison to "at the end of your resources".
- **Punishments as offering.** The text "mentions several of the major punishments": nose-cutting, foot-cutting, branding, castration, imprisonment, exile and death, "becoming an 'offering to the ancestors'". It also suggests the war duties owed to a feudal lord: a noble exiled and oppressed under a tyrant. Philosophically this oppression teaches *de*, "power and virtue", and "the futility of anger and hatred".
- **The Response.** "Make an offering and you will succeed. Wise Words! Trial: the Great Person opens the Way." The words are read as untrustworthy: "If there are words, don't trust them", "Words are not to be trusted".
- **The Scholar Speaks.** Outer relations are cut off from the inner flow: "The mists are outside the stream." Ascending without stopping (46) brought on Confining. Hidden in it is "the possibility of meeting unexpected help".
- **The Shaman Speaks.** "Mists outside the Stream." "Spirit rewards those who suffer in the Pit." Joyous Dancer (Dui) works with the Ghost River (Kan). "This is Metal over Water"; the spirit "dissolves and flows on". "Hold the heart fast and take the risk."
- **Line commentary, briefly.** Line 1: you are your own worst enemy, don't retreat into melancholy. Line 2: the Noble One in exile, "help is on its way". Line 3: "riding a strong line"; you can't even see the friend eager to help. Line 4: "allies will come", slowly. Line 5: "Acquiesce in the blessing"; as bitter feelings loosen "you will be set free". Line 6: "Stop indulging yourself!"; groaning brings only lamentation.

## Notable divergences

1. **臀 tún, line 1.** Legge has "bare buttocks" and adds that the symbolism "is rather a favourite with the duke of Kâu;—'chacun à son goût.'" He reads it as sitting on a stump. Wen makes it a punishment: "Caned on the backside". The usual modern reading (Wilhelm, as quoted) is that one *sits* under a bare tree. Wen's reading ties line 1 to the corporal punishments of line 5, so the six lines become one story of judicial ordeal.
2. **紱 fú, lines 2 and 5.** Legge has red (朱) "knee-covers" in line 2 for the ruler and scarlet (赤) "aprons" in line 5 for nobles or ministers, and he notes the difference in colour. Wen gives vermilion "sash" for both and explains it as the cord for official seals. Legge's version marks rank by colour; Wen's makes the item stand for the bureaucracy itself.
3. **The title.** Legge glosses "straitened and distressed", Wilhelm has "Oppression (Exhaustion)", and Wen has "Blockade". "Exhaustion" points to the image, a lake with no water. "Blockade" points to the glyph, a tree in an enclosure, and to its use in magic. Wen alone mentions that use, and she gives a remedy (Wood qi, Thunder, line 5 changing to 40 Jie).
4. **有言不信, the judgment.** Legge, following the Khang-hsî editors' change of one character, gives "his words cannot be made good": actions are needed, not speech. The literal reading, which Legge gives in his note and Wilhelm uses, is "will not be believed". Wen gives a paradox: "Words spoken, but there is no meaning."
5. **Line 1, 三歲不覿.** Legge has "no prospect (of deliverance)" for a long time. Wen has "Nothing is seen and unseen", and adds a Jupiter/harvest gloss on 歲 that only she gives.
6. **Line 6, 征吉.** Legge: good fortune in "going forward" after repenting. Wen: "Gains from undertaking the expedition", read as a military campaign.
7. **Tarot.** D'Aoust assigns 47 the **Four of Cups**. This week's readings follow the Golden Dawn decans, where the 4 of Cups is Moon in Cancer III and the 5 of Cups is Mars in Scorpio I (the Scorpio I card that came up this week). Her tarot correspondences are her own and do not follow the decan scheme. Both cards are Cups: the 4 shows a seated figure refusing what is offered, the 5 mourns spilled cups. Both suit her "pity party" reading.
8. **Prison or garden.** Legge, Wen and Wilhelm all treat 47 as real constraint. D'Aoust reverses it, reading the enclosure as a "Secret Garden" and the prison as self-made by one's attitude.
9. **Bastian's bluntness.** Every other source finds some way out (Wen's Thunder remedy, D'Aoust's garden, Legge's good end for the great man). Bastian calls 47 "a primary danger sign", and renewed strength from extremity "is the best that can be said".
10. **紱 fu resolved.** Huang gives both senses: a seal-ribbon (Wen's "sash") *and* a knee-covering ceremonial robe (Legge's "knee-covers", his own "kneecaps"). The sash vs knee-covers divergence (item 2) is two halves of one word.
11. **臀 again.** Huang, like Legge, has "Buttocks", and explains why: the bottom line is usually the toes, but here the subject is sitting. He thus stands with the "sits" reading against Wen's "caned". He also softens 三歲 to "not necessarily three years", where Wen reads it as Jupiter-years.
12. **Line 5's exhaustion.** Wen reads the punishments as a sentence passed. Legge has ministers in scarlet aprons. Huang has a ruler hemmed in by yin officials: exhaustion that is neither lack nor excess, but an aspiration blocked.
13. **The carriage.** 金車 is Legge's "carriage adorned with metal", Wen's and Huang's "golden carriage", and Karcher's "**bronze war-chariot**". 金 means metal or gold. Karcher's reading uses the Bronze Age meaning and fits his feudal war-duty frame. Huang's goes the other way: the prince's carriage going to greet the bride.
14. **囗 or 口.** Huang reads the enclosure as 口 *kou*, "big mouth". Karcher reads it as 囗 *wei*, enclosure or old house. Both keep the "big mouth" image. 困 is in fact written with 囗.
15. **Line 6's ending.** Legge and Huang say moving forward brings good fortune, and Wen says "Gains from undertaking the expedition". Karcher has "Chastising opens the Way", which reverses line 2's "Chastising closes the Way". The pair 2/6 frames the hexagram as a way that closes and then opens.
16. **紱 again.** Karcher has scarlet (line 2) and **crimson** (line 5) *sashes*. That keeps Legge's colour distinction (朱 vs 赤) together with Wen's "sash".

## Speculative links to this week's readings

*Interpretation only. None of this is in the sources.*

- **歲 = Jupiter.** Wen glosses line 1's three 歲 as Jupiter-years and harvests. Jupiter rules the third decan of Libra in the Chaldean decan scheme. Tarot gives that decan to the 4 of Swords (see [[reading-2026-10-18]]), and major arcanum X, the Wheel of Fortune, is Jupiter's card ([[reading-2026-10-20]], 20 Oct; the 22 Oct Justice/47 reading is in [[john-paul-ii]]). So the "three years" in the dark valley could be read as Jupiter's turnings, a wheel that has to complete its cycles before the blockade lifts.
- **A tree in an enclosure, and Carcer.** The geomantic figure **Carcer** ("Prison", Saturn) is drawn in `cli/geomancy.py` as bits (1,0,0,1), solid at both ends and open in the middle: an enclosure, like the 口 around 木. Its meanings in the CLI ("Delay, binding, bar, restriction") match Wen's "Blockade" closely.
- **Tai Xuan 69 Exhaustion.** In `cli/taixuan.py`, `TETRAGRAM_NAMES[68]` = "Exhaustion" (tetragram 69, counting from 1), and `represent_tetragram(68)` = `[2, 1, 1, 2]`, i.e. bottom to top **Man, Earth, Earth, Man**. That is checked against the code. Man (the Em mediator line) frames two Earth lines, the same outer-holds-inner shape as Carcer and 困. Tetragram 69 has the same name as Wilhelm's subtitle for hexagram 47. Read as a picture, a doubled middle is boxed in by two mediating lines.
- **The unicorn is the glyph.** *(Speculative.)* In *The Unicorn in Captivity* the unicorn is tied to a tree (木) inside a round fence (口). The tapestry is almost a picture of 困, which D'Aoust shows as a branching figure inside a "Magic Circle". Her personal example, a plant in an indoor enclosure, is the same picture.
- **Magic circle or blockade.** *(Speculative.)* In Wen, 47 is used in baneful magic to *blockade* someone, and the enclosure is a hostile working. In D'Aoust it is a "Magic Circle", which in Western ritual magic is the operator's own protective boundary. The same box is read from the inside (sanctuary, "secret garden") and from the outside (binding).

- **Karcher's Paradigm and this site.** *(Speculative.)* In Karcher's system, the four enclosure figures on these pages (困, Carcer → 39, 28, 29) all lie on the 47:48 Paradigm grid. His two closing lines ("mutual meeting" and "interconnecting through the source") read like a gloss on the syzygy itself: two zones that meet through a shared current.
