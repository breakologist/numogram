## The core

- [Wilhelm] Water over fire: K'an the Abysmal above, Li the Clinging below. Wilhelm reads the figure as the evolution of Peace (11). The move from confusion to order is already complete, and "everything is in its proper place even in particulars": strong lines in strong places, weak lines in weak ones. That looks favourable, "yet it gives reason for thought." The warning is the heart of the hexagram: "it is just when perfect equilibrium has been reached that any movement may cause order to revert to disorder." If the lines go on moving each according to its nature, Standstill (12) appears again at once. So the hexagram describes "a time of climax, which necessitate[s] the utmost caution."
- [Wilhelm] The Judgment: "AFTER COMPLETION. Success in small matters. / Perseverance furthers. / At the beginning good fortune, / At the end disorder." The turn from the old time to the new is already accomplished. In principle everything "stands systematized," and "it is only in regard to details that success is still to be achieved." The commentary then warns that when everything seems to proceed of its own accord, it is easy to relax and stop troubling over details. "Such indifference is the root of all evil," and decay follows. That is "the usual course of history," but not an inescapable law: unremitting perseverance and caution can still avoid it.
- [Wilhelm] The Image: "Water over fire: the image of the condition / In AFTER COMPLETION. / Thus the superior man / Takes thought of misfortune / And arms himself against it in advance." When water in a kettle hangs over fire, the two elements stand in relation and generate energy (Wilhelm notes steam). The tension demands caution: if the water boils over, the fire is extinguished; if the heat is too great, the water evaporates. The elements, "by nature hostile to each other," are useful only with "the most extreme caution." When all forces seem in balance, "only the sage recognizes the moments that bode danger" and banishes it by timely precautions.

## The lines, briefly

1. **Wheels and a wet tail.** "He brakes his wheels. / He gets his tail in the water. / No blame." After a great transition everything presses forward and, at the beginning, "overshoots the mark." A strong character checks his course in time and is hit only from behind, "like a fox that, having crossed the water, at the last minute gets its tail wet."
2. **○ The lost curtain.** "The woman loses the curtain of her carriage. / Do not run after it; / On the seventh day you will get it." Wilhelm marks this line with ○. The curtain hid her from curious glances; to drive on without it was a breach of propriety. In public life this is the confidence of the authorities, withheld when those in power grow arrogant and stop fostering new talent. One is not to throw oneself away seeking it. Wait; "that which is a man's own cannot be permanently lost."
3. **The Illustrious Ancestor.** "The Illustrious Ancestor / Disciplines the Devil's Country. / After three years he conquers it. / Inferior people must not be employed." This is the dynastic title of Emperor Wu Ting of the Yin dynasty. A footnote dates the reign: "Wu Ting reigned from 1324 to 1266 B.C." [Wilhelm, p. 247]. After completion, colonial expansion almost inevitably follows; the territory won at bitter cost must not be treated as an almshouse for people who have "made themselves impossible at home." The same danger, Wilhelm says, belongs to every ambitious undertaking.
4. **Clothes to rags.** "The finest clothes turn to rags. / Be careful all day long." In a flowering culture a convulsion uncovers hidden evil, causes a sensation, and is then glossed over until peace seems to return. The thoughtful man treats such things as grave omens and does not neglect them. *Earlier slip (unchecked gist):* this line was rendered as "fine stuff, and patches ready," which is closer to the received Chinese 繻有衣袽 than to Wilhelm's English, and the commentary was taken as if it were about keeping rags ready to patch a leak. Book III shows the source of that reading: see Chêng Tzŭ, below.
5. **The small offering.** "The neighbor in the east who slaughters an ox / Does not attain as much real happiness / As the neighbor in the west / With his small offering." After completion, worship grows elaborate and showy, but "inner seriousness is lacking." "While man sees what is before his eyes, God looks into the heart," and "a simple sacrifice offered with real piety holds a greater blessing than an impressive service without warmth."
6. **The head in the water.** "He gets his head in the water. Danger." After crossing a stream, the head enters the water "only if he is so imprudent as to turn back." He escapes by going forward and not looking back. The risk is "a fascination in standing still and looking back on a peril overcome"; "vain self-admiration brings misfortune" unless one resolves to go forward without pausing. *Earlier slip (unchecked gist):* the line was glossed as "looking back, or going on past the end," which adds a caution that is the opposite of Wilhelm's: he escapes precisely by going forward.


## Book III

- [Wilhelm] This is "the only one in which all the lines stand in their proper places." The primary trigrams are K'an over Li; the nuclear trigrams are the same two, in reverse order, Li and K'an. The ruler is the six in the second place, weak, but in correspondence with the strong nine in the fifth. AFTER COMPLETION "means making firm."
- [Wilhelm] The equilibrium is "obviously unstable": "K'an strives downward and Li upward." The Commentary on the Decision puts the warning sharply: "If one stands still at the end, disorders arise, because the way comes to an end." When no further progress is possible, "disorder necessarily arises, because the way cannot go on."
- [Wilhelm] In the Image, fire and water counteract each other, which both creates an equilibrium and suggests the fear of a collapse. K'an is danger and disaster, Li is clarity and foresight. "The taking thought occurs in the heart, the arming in external actions."
- [Wilhelm] The line positions carry a tight trigram symbolism, which is part of the pleasure of this commentary. K'an is wheel, fox, hindering, wagon, robbers, the pig of the small sacrifice, the north, and, in the Sequence of Earlier Heaven, the west. Li is curtain, weapons, ox, the middle daughter, and the east, standing opposite K'an.
- [Wilhelm] The fox runs the length of the hexagram. Line 1 is "at the rear of the fox, hence the tail"; the six at the top "is its head." The head enters the water because it is a weak line at the top of K'an; "while crossing the water it turns back and so incurs the danger of drowning." These are "the disorders prophesied by the hexagram as the final outcome."
- [Wilhelm] At line 2, "after seven days" is "the complete cycle of change in the six lines of the hexagram; at the seventh change the starting point recurs." The curtain belongs to Li, the theft to K'an as robbers and wagon. The line stands between two strong lines, like a woman who has lost her veil, yet because she is correct she comes to no harm and the veil returns.
- [Wilhelm] Line 4 in Book III still has Wilhelm's own "finest clothes turn to rags," a yielding line entering K'an. Then "Chêng Tzŭ gives another explanation. He employs the image of a boat, and says: 'It has a leak, but there are rags for plugging it up.'" That is the commentarial reading the earlier, unchecked gist had followed.
- [Wilhelm] Line 5 sets the neighbours in Earlier Heaven: the second line, in nuclear K'an, is the western neighbour, bringing the lesser offering of a pig at the right time; the fourth, in nuclear Li, is the eastern neighbour, bringing the ox but "not central." The nine in the fifth place presides over the sacrifice.

## Cross-links [speculation]

- [repo] Zhu Zhen's Song-dynasty chart pairs Tai Xuan 73 成 Completion with KW 63 (「成，既濟也」). 71 and 72 sit with 52; 78 On the Verge sits with 64.
- Line 1 of 73, a completion that looks unfinished and so keeps working, sits beside 63's leftover work in the details, and beside the fox already across, its tail wet only at the last minute.
- Line 7 of 73, 成闕補, patch the gap, sits beside the Image: taking thought of misfortune and arming oneself in advance, and beside Chêng Tzŭ's boat, which has a leak but rags for plugging it up.
- The top of 73, 君子以終, and 63's "at the end disorder": a page may be taken as complete, then seen as hardened, and may then become a seed. After Completion is not a stop. It is the moment when the form is there and must either be kept from slipping or, as the top line has it, carried forward without the vanity of looking back.
- A period of beginnings, as the user put it, already implies that something has passed, a ruminative or digestive interval that makes way for the new. That sits beside the seventh change, where after the six lines have run their course "the starting point recurs," and beside the thought that some distance from an ending is what lets one take the work up again. Standing still at the end is the danger Book III names; the recurrence is not a halt, but a return.
