# Hexagram 63 既濟 Chi Chi / After Completion

*by Em (in collaboration with the user), 13 Nov 2026.* A paraphrase of Wilhelm/Baynes, Book I, made while 63 was the gua qi hexagram beside Tai Xuan 73 Completion (gua qi from 9 Nov, the tetragram from 10 Nov). Everything is paraphrased unless it is quoted. Tags: [Wilhelm], [repo], [speculation].

> **Verification note (Em, 13 Nov 2026):** The user's photographs cover Book I pp. 244–248. Only p. 244 was available for line-by-line checking in this pass. Quotations on this page are taken only from p. 244. The Image and the six lines are given as a gist of what those later pages contain, not as checked extracts. The private study transcription will be finished when the remaining photographs are to hand.

## The core

- [Wilhelm] Water over fire: K'an the Abysmal above, Li the Clinging below. Wilhelm reads the figure as the evolution of Peace (11). The move from confusion to order is already complete, and "everything is in its proper place even in particulars": strong lines in strong places, weak lines in weak ones. That looks favourable, "yet it gives reason for thought." The warning is the heart of the hexagram: "it is just when perfect equilibrium has been reached that any movement may cause order to revert to disorder." If the lines go on moving each according to its nature, Standstill (12) appears again at once. So the hexagram describes "a time of climax, which necessitate[s] the utmost caution."
- [Wilhelm] The Judgment: "AFTER COMPLETION. Success in small matters. / Perseverance furthers. / At the beginning good fortune, / At the end disorder." The turn from the old time to the new is already accomplished. In principle everything "stands systematized," and "it is only in regard to details that success is still to be achieved." The commentary continues onto the next page.
- [Wilhelm] The Image is water over fire, read as a kettle: the two elements are in use together, which is exactly when the arrangement can fail, so one thinks of harm and prepares for it. This is the gist of the later pages, not a checked quotation.

## The lines, briefly

These are the striking images from the later photographs, given as a gist only.

1. **The wet tail.** A crossing already under way, the fox's tail in the water. Caution at the start, and no blame.
2. **○ The lost curtain.** The carriage-curtain is lost; it is not to be chased, and it comes back on the seventh day. Wilhelm marks this line with ○.
3. **Wu Ting.** A long campaign against the Devil's Country, won only after three years; inferior people are not to be used. A footnote dates Wu Ting's reign 1324–1266 B.C. [Wilhelm, p. 247].
4. **Rags to hand.** Fine stuff, and patches ready; caution all day. The received line is 繻有衣袽，終日戒.
5. **The small offering.** A neighbour's slaughtered ox counts for less than a small, sincere offering, which actually receives the blessing.
6. **The head in the water.** Looking back, or going on past the end, puts the head in the water, and there is danger.

## Cross-links [speculation]

- [repo] Zhu Zhen's Song-dynasty chart pairs Tai Xuan 73 成 Completion with KW 63 (「成，既濟也」). 71 and 72 sit with 52; 78 On the Verge sits with 64.
- Line 1 of 73, a completion that looks unfinished and so keeps working, sits beside 63's leftover work in the details, and beside the wet tail that is already in the crossing.
- Line 7 of 73, 成闕補, patch the gap, sits beside the precaution that belongs to a finished state: the kettle, the rags, the caution at the climax.
- The top of 73, 君子以終, and 63's "at the end disorder": a page may be taken as complete, then seen as hardened, and may then become a seed. After Completion is not a stop. It is the moment when the form is there and must either be kept from slipping or be allowed to start again.
