## 1. The Prince of Cups

**Book T** (Mathers and Felkin, Golden Dawn). The card is titled "XI. The Prince of the Chariot of the Waters", and "He rules from 20 Degree Libra to 20 Degree Scorpio." It is "Air of Water" and "Prince and Emperor of Nymphs or Undines".

- **Imagery:** "seated in a chariot drawn by an eagle. On the wheel is the symbol of a scorpion." Also: "Beneath his chariot is the calm and stagnant water of a lake." Also: "A serpent issues from the cup, and has its head tending down to the waters of the lake."
- **Character:** "He is subtle, violent, crafty and artistic; a fierce nature with calm exterior. Powerful for good or evil but more attracted by the evil if allied with apparent Power or Wisdom."

**Crowley, *The Book of Thoth*** (1944), as transcribed at book-of-thoth.net:

- **The hidden scorpion:** "The third totem, the scorpion, is not shewn in the picture, for the putrefaction which it represents is an extremly secret process." (The transcription spells it "extremly".)
- **Character:** "He is intensely secret, an artist in all his ways. On the surface he appears calm and imperturble, but this is a mask of the most intense passion." (The transcription spells it "imperturble".)
- **Libra into Scorpio:** "Libra going over into Scorpio is of tremendous, active, critical energy and weight."
- **Yi King:** Crowley's hexagram for the card is **61, not 36**: "In the Yi King, the airy part of Water is represented by the 61st hexagram, Kung Fû."

**Today's position.** The Sun at 26° Libra lies inside the card's 20° Libra – 20° Scorpio span. This comes from the calendar's computed solar longitude and the Book T span.

## 2. Hexagram 36 — Ming Yi ([[hexagram-zone-mapping]]) (Legge, *Sacred Books of the East* vol. 16, 1882)

**Text and lines:**
- **Judgement:** "Ming Î indicates that (in the circumstances which it denotes) it will be advantageous to realise the difficulty (of the position), and maintain firm correctness."
- **Line 2:** "wounded in the left thigh. He saves himself by the strength of a (swift) horse; and is fortunate."
- **Line 5:** "shows how the count of K î fulfilled the condition indicated by Ming Î." Kî is Jizi, 箕子.
- **Line 6:** "there is no light, but (only) obscurity … his future shall be to go into the earth."
- **Legge's note on the name:** "Ming Î, or 'Intelligence Wounded,' that is, injured and repressed". He describes the figure as "a good and intelligent minister or officer going forward in the service of his country, notwithstanding the occupancy of the throne by a weak and unsympathising sovereign".

**The two stories** (Appendix I, Tuan commentary):
- **King Wen:** "The case of king Wăn was that of one who with these qualities was yet involved in great difficulties."
- **Hiding one's brightness:** "(the individual concerned) should obscure his brightness."
- **Prince Ji:** "The case of the count of K î was that of one who, amidst the difficulties of his House, was able (thus) to maintain his aim and mind correct."

**Image** (Appendix II): "The superior man, in accordance with this, conducts his management of men;--he shows his intelligence by keeping it obscured." On line 5: "his brightness could not be (quite) extinguished."

**Jizi** (Wikipedia, citing the classical sources): "The earliest known mention of Jizi is in the 'Mingyi' 明夷 hexagram of the Book of Change." Also: "Jizi was either imprisoned or enslaved for remonstrating against King Zhou's misrule." And: "(One later version states that he pretended to be mad after Bigan had been killed by King Zhou.)"

## 3. St John de Brébeuf (1593–1649)

**Catholic Encyclopedia** (T. J. Campbell, 1907, New Advent):

- **Life:** he was "born at Condé-sur-Vire in Normandy, 25 March, 1593; died in Canada, near Georgian Bay, 16 March, 1649".
- **Early mission:** "He immediately took up his abode in the Indian wigwams". His later labours among the Hurons are described as "a continual series of privations and sufferings which he used to say were only roses in comparison with what the end was to be."
- **Capture:** Brébeuf and Lalemant "could have escaped but rejected the offer made to them and remained with their flock."
- **Martyrdom at St Ignace:** "Brébeuf had scalding water poured on his head in mockery of baptism". Also: "Through all the torture he never uttered a groan."

**Jesuit Relations** (Thwaites, vol. XII, 1898; Le Jeune's Relation of 1637, which prints Brébeuf's "instructions for the Fathers of our Society who shall be sent to the Hurons"):

- "You must have sincere affection for the Savages, looking upon them as ransomed by the blood of the son of God, and as our brethren, with whom we are to pass the rest of our lives." The wording, including "Savages", is the 1898 translation of the 1637 text.
- The same volume, in Le Jeune's account of Huron seminarists, records a Wendat tradition "of a very remarkable darkening of the Sun, which was supposed to have happened because the great turtle which upholds the earth, in changing its position or place, brought its shell before the Sun".

**Huron Carol.** Wikipedia (citing McGee 1985) says it was "written probably in 1642 by Jean de Brébeuf", and that its Huron title is "Jesous Ahatonhia" ("Jesus, he is born").
- The familiar English, "'Twas in the moon of winter-time", is **J. E. Middleton's 1926 version, not Brébeuf's**. It includes "Before their light the stars grew dim".
- The translation of the Wendat text quoted on Wikipedia opens: "Have courage, you who are humans. Jesus, He is born. / Behold, it has fled, the spirit who had us as prisoner."

## Links

### Sourced (each claim rests on a fetched text; none of the sources links all three)

1. **No source links the three figures.** They meet only because the calendar puts them together:
   - the Prince of Cups, through the Sun's position in its span;
   - Ming Yi, through Meng Xi's gua qi for the date;
   - Brébeuf, through the liturgical calendar.

   Crowley's hexagram for the Prince of Cups is 61, not 36.
2. **Hidden light under hostile rule** is the explicit theme of Ming Yi in Legge's text and appendices: "obscure his brightness"; "keeping it obscured"; King Wen; the count of Kî.
3. **"a fierce nature with calm exterior"** (Book T) and **"a mask of the most intense passion"** (Crowley): the sources make the Prince of Cups a figure of concealed intensity.
4. **Brébeuf chose to stay in danger** among the Wendat ("remained with their flock") and **endured in silence** ("never uttered a groan"). He wrote a carol whose English version uses light imagery, though that version is Middleton's.
5. **A darkening of the sun** appears in Wendat tradition as recorded in the same 1637 Relation that carries Brébeuf's Instructions.

### Speculative

- **Ming Yi's officer and the missionary.** Legge's officer "going forward in the service" under an unsympathetic sovereign fits Brébeuf's years in "constant danger", but the cases are not parallel. Ming Yi counsels obscuring one's brightness, whereas Brébeuf's mission was to proclaim his. A closer fit is Ming Yi's endurance: "his brightness could not be (quite) extinguished".
- **Calm surface, fire beneath.** Book T's "calm and stagnant water of a lake", with a serpent heading down into it, sits beside Brébeuf's silence at the stake. That is an image-rhyme, not a source.
- **Water and Scorpio.** The Prince of Cups' Scorpio (the putrefaction "not shewn") and Ming Yi's sun "go into the earth" both describe a descent into hiding. Brébeuf's mock baptism with scalding water is a grim inversion of water imagery.
- **Libra, Justice and the Wheel.** The decan's trumps are Justice and Fortune. Jizi's remonstrance and imprisonment, and Brébeuf's choice to stay, can both be read as conscience under a turning wheel.

## Tai Xuan 68 — 瞢 Dimming (Sima Guang, *Jizhu Taixuan jing*; Shidianguji transcription)

- **Head text:** "瞢陰征南，陽征北，物失明貞，莫不瞢瞢。" My rough gloss: *Yin marches south, yang marches north; things lose their bright constancy; none is not dimmed.*
- **Today's appraisal 次二:** "次二，明腹睒天，睹其根。測曰：眀腹睒天，中獨爛也。" My rough gloss: *Brightness within [or: returning] glimpses heaven and sees its root. Fathoming: the centre alone is radiant.*
- **Caveat on the text:** this is an OCR transcription. The Fan Wang edition, also on Shidianguji, reads "明复" (simplified in that transcription; *brightness returns*) where this one has "明腹" (*brightness in the belly*). I have not checked a published translation, such as Nylan's.

*(Speculative.)* Either reading suits the day: inner light that is hidden but still sees its root, beside Ming Yi's sun under the earth.
